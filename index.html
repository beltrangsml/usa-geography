The Headline

One piece of news from 2026 that only makes sense once you look at a map.

Breaking · New York · 22 September 2026

US, Denmark and Greenland sign Arctic security deal

Signed on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly after almost two years in which Washington repeatedly demanded control of the island, a crisis that strained relations with European NATO allies [S2, S3, S4].

New US sites

Two new defence areas, Narsarsuaq and Mestersvig, and an expanded Pituffik Space Base [S3, S5].

Access

Overflight, landing and undersea rights across Greenland, including territorial waters [S3].

Exclusion

No non-NATO military presence, and a veto on sensitive investment from outside NATO and the EU [S3, S4].

Limits

No end date and no transfer of sovereignty; it still needs the Danish and Greenlandic parliaments [S3, S6].

II Act II · theoretical framework

Marshall’s Lens

Before answering the questions, the theory we use to read the map.

The thesis

The physical landscape (rivers, mountains, seas, climate, resources) constrains and enables what leaders can do.

Geography does not decide everything, but every decision is taken within its limits. Technology “bends” some rules without abolishing them (Introduction).

MARSHALL [S1]
Applied to the United States

“Location, location, location”: a two-ocean superpower.

A series of shrewd territorial decisions let the country control a continent uniquely favourable to power: fertile land, the best network of navigable rivers, natural harbours and two oceans as moats.

MARSHALL · CH. 3 [S1]
  1. Secure the coreAtlantic seaboard and the Mississippi basin (Louisiana, 1803).
  2. Reach both oceans1819 treaty, Texas 1845, Mexican War 1846–48, California.
  3. Remove rivalsMonroe Doctrine 1823, war with Spain 1898, Panama.
  4. Control the approachesBlue-water navy, Destroyers for Bases 1940, Pacific bases.
  5. Project powerNATO 1949, Asian alliances, managing rivals.
The tradition behind Marshall

Our addition, not Marshall’s argument: he lists Mackinder in his bibliography, but Mahan and Spykman do not appear in the book [S1].

1890 · SEA

Mahan

Control of the sea made Britain the world power [S58].

His ideas fed the Naval Act of 1890 and the push for a canal; Theodore Roosevelt admired him [S59].

Marshall’s steps 3–4: two coasts become power only with a navy that holds the Caribbean, Hawaii and Panama.
1904 · HEARTLAND

Mackinder

“Who rules East Europe commands the Heartland” (1919) [S60].

He placed the Americas, Britain and Japan in an “outer crescent” reached by sea [S61].

Why a country safe behind two oceans must still stop one power ruling Eurasia.
1938 · RIMLAND

Spykman

“Geography is the most fundamental factor in foreign policy because it is the most permanent” [S62].

The rim of Eurasia matters most; he urged bases in Japan and the Philippines [S63]. He also rejected strict determinism [S62].

Marshall’s step 5: NATO, Japan, South Korea and the Philippines ring Eurasia.
VERIFIED FACT [S58–S63] · OUR ANALYSIS
How we use himMarshall is a journalist, and his approach is often called geographical determinism. Acemoglu and Robinson (2012) argue that institutions explain prosperity better than geography; Kaplan (2012) and Zeihan (2014) defend geography’s weight. We adopt the framework and test it. Our position: geography is the stage and the constraint, not the script.

Act IV · Part 2 · critical counterpoint

But Geography Did Not Sign the Deal

Greenland’s 2026 outcome was decided by diplomacy, alliance politics and parliaments, not by geography alone.

What holds up

Largest military spender ($954 bn, 33% of the world, 2025) [S15] and largest oil producer (13.6 m b/d) [S14]. Marshall’s energy-exporter forecast came true in 2019 [S34].

Where it needs qualification

Brazil and Russia have land without equivalent power (Acemoglu and Robinson). The “empty” continent was not empty. Net exporter does not mean immune: the Hormuz crisis of 2026 [S23].

In the headline itself

No sovereignty was transferred [S6]; the deal needs the Danish and Greenlandic parliaments [S3]; it respects Greenland’s self-determination [S3]. Geography set the stakes; politics set the outcome.

Answer to the research question

History

Harbours drew settlers, the Appalachians held them, the Mississippi unified them, the oceans protected them. At the cost of Native nations, Mexico and the enslaved.

People

Land and distance made a nation of immigrants defined by ideas, mobility and opportunity, and distinct regions like Texas and California.

Power

Geography still underpins US power in 2026, and the Greenland deal shows Washington still thinks in maps. The threats are now political and human.

“Geography made America possible. It does not guarantee America’s future.”

The deal, read again

Each clause through Marshall’s lens and what we now know about the people. Then the three questions from Act I, answered.

Three questions, three answers

1

Why would the world’s strongest power pressure its own ally for an ice-covered island?

Because Greenland sits on the shortest polar route to Russia and flanks the GIUK gap: for a continent protected by two oceans, it is the Arctic gate. OUR ANALYSIS · MARSHALL [S1, S3]
2

Why does Washington believe it can buy, pressure and build its way into new territory?

Because it always has: Louisiana (1803), Alaska (1867) and the Monroe Doctrine taught it that land can be acquired and rivals kept out of the hemisphere. OUR ANALYSIS · MARSHALL [S1]
3

What does this tell us about the people and the country behind the decision?

A frontier, commercial culture that treats territory as a deal, inside a country whose regions and votes are themselves drawn by geography. OUR ANALYSIS [S50, S80]
